# Two Versions and a Coin

Two versions of something — a poster, a sign-up form, a message, a recipe. You want to know which is better.

The instinct is to show both and count. This is the right instinct, and it is much harder to do honestly than it looks.

## The comparison that is not one

Try it first the natural way. Put version A up on Monday and version B up on Tuesday. Count.

B wins. Does B win?

Tuesday is not Monday. Different people were around, in a different mood, with different amounts of time. If the thing is a sign-up sheet in a corridor, Tuesday had an assembly. If it is a message, Tuesday's went out after lunch.

Every one of those differences is now tangled up with the difference between A and B, and nothing in your count can separate them. You have measured "B on Tuesday" against "A on Monday", and you wanted to measure B against A.

This is not a technicality. It is the entire problem, and no amount of collecting more data fixes it — a bigger count of the wrong comparison is a more precise wrong answer.

## What the coin is for

The fix is to decide, for each person, at random, which version they see.

Flip a coin. Heads they see A, tails they see B. Both versions run at the same time, to the same kind of people, under the same conditions.

Now the only systematic difference between the two groups is the thing you changed. Everything else — mood, weather, time of day, who happened to walk past — got split roughly evenly by the coin, because that is what randomness does. It does not eliminate those factors. It makes them fall equally on both sides, which is just as good and is the only thing available.

This is one of the genuinely great ideas, and it is worth appreciating how strange it is: the way to handle all the things you cannot think of is to stop trying to think of them and let a coin distribute them for you. You do not need to know what they are. You do not need to measure them. The randomness handles the ones you never imagined, which is the majority.

## The part everybody skips

Before you look at any results, write down two things.

**How many observations you will collect.** A number, fixed in advance.

**What you are measuring, and what result would change your mind.** Not "see which is better" — a specific quantity, and a threshold.

Write these somewhere you cannot edit. A photo of a piece of paper works. This will feel like bureaucracy. The next chapter is about why it is the difference between a real result and a decorated opinion.

## What a difference has to beat

Suppose A gets 12 sign-ups and B gets 15. Is B better?

Before answering: if you had flipped a coin for every person and *shown them all the same version*, would you expect exactly equal counts? Obviously not. You would expect something like 13 and 14, or 11 and 16, or occasionally 9 and 18. Random splits do not come out even.

So the question is never "is B ahead?" — B will be ahead about half the time by luck alone. The question is "is B far enough ahead that luck is an unconvincing explanation?"

You can feel this out physically, and it is worth doing once rather than being told. Take a coin. Flip it forty times, recording heads and tails in two columns. You know the coin is fair. Look at the gap anyway. Do it again. Do it ten times and write down the biggest gap you saw.

Now you have a personal sense of how big a gap luck produces with forty flips. When your real experiment gives you 12 against 15 out of forty, you can compare it against that. Usually the honest answer is: that is well inside what the coin does on its own.

Every formal test of significance is a mechanised version of exactly this comparison. The formula is faster and more precise. It answers the same question you just answered with a coin and a tally, and if you have done it with the coin first, the formula is a convenience rather than an oracle.

## Smaller effects need more people

One consequence follows immediately, and it explains most disappointing experiments.

If B is enormously better than A, a few dozen observations will show it — the gap will be far outside what luck produces. If B is slightly better, the gap it produces is the same size as the gaps luck produces, and no amount of staring will separate them. You need more observations, and "more" grows fast: halving the size of the effect you want to detect roughly quadruples the number of people you need.

So decide in advance how big a difference would actually matter to you, and work out whether you can realistically collect enough observations to see one. If you cannot, the honest thing is to say the experiment cannot answer the question — before running it, rather than after, when the temptation to find something will be much stronger.

That temptation is the subject of the next chapter, and it is the one that catches nearly everybody.
