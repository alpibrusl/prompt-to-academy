# A Chart Is a Claim

Find a chart in a news story. Redraw it with the vertical axis starting at zero and the full time range shown.

Compare the two pictures. Quite often it is not the same story.

## A chart is an argument that looks like an observation

This is the thing to internalise, and everything else follows from it.

A photograph feels like evidence. A chart feels like a photograph of data. It is not. Every chart is the result of a long series of choices — what to include, what to leave out, where to start the axis, how to bin the categories, which comparison to put side by side, what to call things — and every one of those choices can change the impression while every single number stays true.

A chart is a claim. It is an argument, made visually, and it can be a dishonest argument composed entirely of accurate figures.

## The four choices that do most of the work

**Where the axis starts.** A vertical axis starting at 98 instead of 0 turns a 2% variation into a mountain range. Nothing is false. Every point is where it belongs. The impression is off by an order of magnitude.

Sometimes a non-zero axis is right — body temperature, where the interesting range really is 36 to 40, and starting at zero would hide everything that matters. The rule is not "always start at zero". It is *know which you did, and know what it does to the reader.*

**Where the time range starts and stops.** Almost any trend can be produced by choosing the window. Start just after a dip and everything rises. Start just before a spike and everything falls. The full available range is the honest default, and any narrower window should come with a reason that is not "it looked better".

**What is being compared to what.** Raw counts where rates belong. A bigger city has more of nearly everything; that is not a finding. Absolute numbers where a proportion is the question, or a proportion where the absolute size is what matters.

**What was left out.** The hardest to spot, because it is not on the page. The category folded into "other". The years that are missing. The group that was excluded. Nothing signals an omission, which is exactly why it works.

## Reading one in thirty seconds

A short routine, in order:

Read the axes first — both of them, including the units, before looking at the shape. The shape is designed to be read first and will colour everything after it.

Find zero. Is it on the chart? If not, why not?

Check the range. Where does it start, where does it stop, and is there an obvious reason for both?

Ask what is missing. What category, what time period, what group would you expect to see that is not here?

Ask what the title claims, and whether the chart shows that. Titles routinely assert causes for pictures that show only that two things moved together.

Only then look at the shape.

That last instruction is the hard one and the valuable one. The shape is the most persuasive thing on the page and the least informative, and by the time you have absorbed it you are reading the axes to confirm an impression you already have.

## Making them honestly

The same list, used forwards.

Start at zero unless you have a reason, and if you have a reason, say it on the chart. Show the full range you have, or state what you cut and why. Label the axes with units. Do not use area or volume to represent a single number — doubling the width of a circle quadruples its area and quadruples the impression. Put the uncertainty on the chart if there is any, because a line drawn confidently through noisy points is a claim about precision that the data cannot support.

And the one that takes discipline: **draw the version that does not favour your conclusion, and look at it.** If the honest version does not make your point, your point may not be there.

This is the same move as estimating before you compute and writing your plan before you look. The pattern in this book is always the same — commit to something *before* the moment when your preferences can quietly shape the outcome, because afterwards you will not be able to tell that they did.

## Three honest versions

A good exercise, and a better habit: take one dataset and draw it three different honest ways. All true. All differently persuasive.

Then argue about which is the fairest representation.

That argument is the actual skill. It has no formula and no correct answer, and the ability to have it well — to say *this one is technically accurate and will mislead the reader, and here is why* — is not something that can be delegated, because it is a judgement about an audience rather than a fact about the data.

It is also, usefully, the best possible preparation for reading a chart that somebody else made on purpose.

## Where this comes from

Truncated axes, cherry-picked windows, raw counts standing in for rates — every dishonest chart in this chapter is a technique with its own long history in newspapers, annual reports, and advertising, well before anyone could generate one by typing a sentence.

What has changed is the cost of producing one. A misleading chart used to take a person who understood the data and chose to mislead with it. Now it can be the accidental output of asking for "a chart of this" with no further thought, because the tool has to make the same four choices — the axis, the window, the comparison, the omission — whether or not anyone asked it to make them honestly. The dishonesty got cheaper to produce by accident, not just on purpose, which is the only thing about this chapter that is actually new.
